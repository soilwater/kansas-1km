Kansas soil water, 2002–2026

Kansas Soil Water Atlas

How much water the top two feet of Kansas soil held, every day since 2002, and where, when and for how long it ran short.

Soil Water Processes Lab, Department of Agronomy, Kansas State University · Daily data from Jan. 1, 2002 to Nov. 3, 2026 · Updated Nov. 5, 2026

Every map and chart on this page comes from one number: the fraction of plant available water (FAW) in the top 60 centimeters (2 feet) of soil, calculated each day on a 1-kilometer grid across Kansas. At 0, plants can no longer pull water from the soil; at 1, the profile is full.

To capture both how dry it got and for how long, the atlas counts FAW-days. Each day below FAW 0.5 adds its shortfall to the year's deficit-days, and each day above 0.5 adds its excess to the surplus-days. Ten days at FAW 0.3 make 2 deficit-days, and so do 40 days at 0.45.

Chapter 1Twenty-five years at a glance

Each row is a year and each cell is a week. Kansas soils dry out in summer and fall and recharge from late fall through spring, so most years run red in the second half. The long red bands of 2011–2012 and 2022–2023 are the two great droughts of the record; the wettest springs came in 2007, 2016 and 2019.

Chapter 2The soil reservoir

Multiplying FAW by the water each soil can hold gives the volume of plant available water stored across the state. Full, the top two feet of Kansas soil hold about 30 cubic kilometers (25 million acre-feet). Since 2002 the reservoir has swung between 4 and 29 km³.

Chapter 3Every year since 2002

Each dial is one year, read clockwise from Jan. 1 at the top. The line is the statewide median FAW; orange fills the shortfall below 0.5 and blue the excess above it. The numbers are the year’s deficit-days and surplus-days.

Chapter 4Across the state

Kansas gets nearly three times more rain in the southeast than in the far west (about 430 to 1,200 millimeters a year), and its soils follow. The west carries most of the deficit-days and the east most of the surplus-days, but drought years reach across the whole state.

Chapter 5Droughts

Treating each drought as one connected region of dry soil (FAW below 0.2) in space and time gives 29 events since 2002 that lasted at least four weeks and covered at least 5 percent of Kansas at their peak. Bubble size is the peak area; color is the total deficit, in statewide FAW-days.

About the dataHow the atlas is made

FAW comes from the Kansas 1-km soil moisture model behind the daily map. A daily soil water balance is driven by PRISM rainfall and a seasonal drying rate calibrated from the Kansas Mesonet, run on soil limits from USDA-SSURGO soil texture, and adjusted toward the Mesonet stations each day. The record starts Jan. 1, 2002 and is rerun from the start when the model changes.

Statewide values are area-weighted over the 273,579 pixels of Kansas. Weeks are fixed seven-day periods starting Jan. 1 (week 52 also takes Dec. 30–31), and years are calendar years. Deficit-days and surplus-days use 0.5 as the reference. Stored water is FAW times each pixel's plant available water capacity (water held between −10 kPa and the wilting point), summed over the state.
